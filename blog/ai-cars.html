AUTOMOTIVE TECHFIELD NOTE 04

AI Is Changing Cars — But the Car Is More Than an AI Model

The easiest way to imagine an AI car is to picture a powerful model making decisions. The engineering reality is much larger.

A car is already a computer network

Modern vehicles contain many electronic control units, sensors, communication networks, and software systems. Engine controllers, braking systems, body electronics, infotainment, cameras, radar, and other components can have different responsibilities.

That makes a modern vehicle interesting from a software perspective: it is a distributed computing system that has to operate under physical and safety constraints.

Where AI enters

AI is particularly useful when a system needs to interpret complex sensor information. Computer vision can help detect lanes, vehicles, pedestrians, traffic signs, and other objects. Machine learning can also be used in perception, prediction, and driver-monitoring tasks.

But perception is only one layer. A production system still needs sensors, embedded compute, communication, control logic, safety mechanisms, testing, and carefully defined boundaries around what the system is allowed to do.

ADAS is not the same as autonomy

Driver-assistance features can automate parts of driving while still requiring the human driver to remain responsible and attentive. It is important not to collapse every AI-powered feature into the idea of a fully autonomous vehicle.

The software-defined vehicle

One trend I find interesting is the move toward software-defined vehicles. More functionality can be controlled or improved through software, and the vehicle increasingly resembles a platform rather than a fixed mechanical product.

This creates a natural connection between my interests in AI and software engineering. A useful automotive AI system is not just a model; it is a model integrated into a much larger engineered system.

The stack: sensors → perception → prediction → planning/control → vehicle systems → human interface.

What I want to learn next

I want to understand CAN bus communication, ECU architecture, sensor fusion, computer vision, ADAS pipelines, and eventually how machine-learning models can run efficiently on edge hardware.

That is what makes automotive AI interesting to me: it combines software, AI, electronics, and a physical machine where the output actually matters.
